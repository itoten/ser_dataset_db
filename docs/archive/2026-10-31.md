Basic principles, and whether the DB creates facts of its own. Each decision gives the question, the background, the decision and the reason.

## 0. Basic principles

- **Question.** What is this DB for, what does it promise, and who reads it?
- **Background.** In AI research the scale of data matters, so researchers keep looking for datasets. But dataset information changes fast, lists go stale within a few years, and investigating datasets has become costly. Everyone repeats the same investigation. Nobody can guarantee correct information: people err and AI hallucinates. Most lists give no sources.
- **Decision.**
  - Documents are written in four layers: mission (why), principles (what matters), rules (what to keep, and can be checked), procedures (how).
  - Mission: lower researchers' cost of investigating datasets by collecting up-to-date information on datasets usable for SER as comprehensively as possible, in a database where anyone can check the primary source of every value.
  - Five principles, none ranking above the others: verifiability, coverage, currency, transparency, neutrality.
  - The DB is a tertiary source. It holds no primary information and offers not the truth but the way to it. It gives no judgements. Readers are told to check and cite the primary source, not the DB.
  - Everything is read through AI; the generated views are an aid.
  - Scope: the axis is SER; sentiment counts as broad emotion; intent and similar data are collected as material for SER.
  - If a secondary source points to a primary one, the primary is cited; a secondary source is a compromise for coverage.
  - Consistency is not a principle (it is an implementation method). Human verification is not made the ideal: relying on AI is accepted, and a person's check is a record of work.
- **Reason.** Wikipedia's rule is verifiability, not truth, and it calls itself a tertiary source. Each principle follows from the background: coverage and currency from the cost of investigation, verifiability and transparency from the impossibility of guaranteeing correctness, neutrality from the dependence of "usable" on purpose.

## A-1. Does the DB create facts of its own?

- **Question.** Should every value come from an outside source?
- **Background.** Four kinds of evidence were not outside sources: counts made by the DB's own scripts (`measured`), the maintainer's decisions (`user`), internal notes (`project_doc`) and the original spreadsheet (`sheet`).
- **Decision.** Yes. The DB creates no facts. It counts nothing itself; values that need counting stay empty until the recipe readme states them, and then the readme is cited at a commit on the public fork. Only primary evidence is added; secondary only when the primary cannot be accessed; a re-upload by someone else is secondary. Decisions such as using a dataset move out of the public facts. When sources disagree the value is `conflicting`; the DB does not pick. Ambiguous wording is recorded as written. Human verification is a record of the DB's work, not a source type.
- **Reason.** It follows from verifiability, the tertiary role and neutrality. A script's mistake would otherwise be the DB's responsibility.

## A-3. Separating general from local information

- **Decision.** No overall line is drawn now; it is considered with each later design decision.
- **Reason.** The line moves when a recipe is merged upstream, so designing it all at once would be wasted effort.

## A-4. The prior-work database

- **Decision.** This DB has no dependence on the separate paper database; anything found is removed.
- **Reason.** Dependencies should be few, and that database was a trial.
